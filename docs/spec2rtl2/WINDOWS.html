spec2rtl v2 · the route's vocabulary, drawn and explained

Windows, Not Ghosts

A patient walk through the central idea of the specification route: what a window is, why the specification declares windows instead of inventing shadow state, and how the same small set of pictures explains representatives, bounds, forms, and both inductions. Every figure is drawn from the miss-queue entry whose proofs earned these rules.

1 The three-layer stack

Begin with the problem the whole arrangement solves. A specification and a design speak two different languages. The specification wants to say things like “an occupied slot remembers which request it holds” — sentences about meaning. The design is made of registers, wires, and enables — objects of construction. If the specification talked about registers directly, it would be married to one particular design forever: rename a register, restructure the pipeline, and the specification would be wrong even though the device still behaves perfectly. We want the opposite: one specification that many designs can satisfy.

The route's answer is to build a deliberate three-layer stack. At the top, the specification states its promises over two kinds of things only: the ports (which every implementation must have — they are the interface) and a small set of named views it calls windows. Crucially, the specification declares the windows but never defines them. Think of a declaration the way a programmer thinks of a function signature without a body: the spec says “there exists a view called slot_valid(I,T), meaning slot I holds an outstanding request at time T, and here are my promises about whatever it shows.” Where the information comes from is deliberately left open.

At the bottom sits one particular design — real hardware. And between them sits the linkage: a short, per-design adapter file whose only job is to say which real register each declared window looks at. One line per window. The linkage is the only place in the whole stack where the specification's vocabulary and the design's vocabulary appear in the same sentence.

Follow one promise down the stack to make this concrete. The requirement, stated the way this route wants every requirement stated — as a cause and its consequence:

If a slot is occupied at cycle T and is still occupied at cycle T+1, then the address it shows at T+1 is the same address it showed at T.

In the P → Q form the causal layer uses, the same sentence is:

slot_valid(0, T) && slot_valid(0, T+1)
    -> slot_address(0, T+1) = slot_address(0, T)

Notice what the rule is written over: only the two windows. It does not mention a register. Then a particular design comes along with a register it happens to call address_0, and its linkage adds the one line connecting window to register. If tomorrow a different design stores addresses in a shared array instead of per-slot registers, the same rule is checked unchanged — only that design's linkage line looks different.

Notice who does what. The person writing the specification never meets a register. The person building a design meets registers constantly — and meets the specification's vocabulary exactly once, when writing the linkage. That clean division of labor is the entire point of the stack, and it is what makes Section 4's freedom picture possible.

2 A window is a pane of glass

So what exactly is a window, mechanically? It is a named pane of glass held over some part of the design's state. Through the pane, the specification can see a value; it cannot touch it, and it cannot see anything the pane is not pointed at. Here are three real linkage lines from the miss queue, worth reading slowly:

slot_valid(0, T)      :- val(valid_0, 1, T).
slot_address(0, A, T) :- val(address_0, A, T), val(valid_0, 1, T).
slot_data(0, V, T)    :- val(line_data_0, V, T), val(filled_0, 1, T), val(valid_0, 1, T).

The first line reads: “the window slot_valid(0,T) shows occupied whenever the register valid_0 holds 1 at time T.” Simple mounting — one pane, one register. But look at the second line: the address pane shows the value of the address register only while the valid register also holds 1. And the third goes further: the data pane shows the stored line only while the slot is both valid and filled. Those extra conditions are called scoping, and they are not a stylistic flourish — they are load-bearing, for a reason that comes from the hardware itself.

Here is the hardware reason. In this design, only the valid bit has a reset wire. The address, tag, pointer, and data registers power up holding garbage and are never cleared — clearing them would cost a reset network across hundreds of flops and buy nothing, because a free slot's contents are meaningless anyway. But “meaningless garbage sitting in registers” sounds like exactly the thing that breaks formal proofs: some promise reads the garbage, compares it to something, and produces a spurious counterexample. Scoping is what prevents that, completely: while valid_0 is 0, the second and third linkage lines simply never fire, so there is no atom at all saying what the address or data of slot 0 is. A promise cannot trip over a value that, as far as the logic is concerned, does not exist. The garbage is not “handled” — it is invisible.

One more thing a window buys, almost as a side effect: it is a place to put a meaning. The window's name and prose meaning (“slot I's line address, while it holds one”) belong to the specification and never change. Which register realizes it is the design's business, per design, and can change freely. The pane is the stable thing; the glass moves with the furniture behind it.

3 Why not a ghost? The picture that justifies the rule

To feel why the window discipline matters, walk through what almost everyone tries first — because it is genuinely the natural idea. Suppose you are specifying this miss queue and you think: “the spec should have its own little model of what's outstanding. I'll keep a set: when a request is accepted, add it; when a fill arrives, mark its waiters; when a delivery happens, remove one. Then my promises just compare the design against my model.” That invented, spec-side state — the model set, a shadow queue, a scoreboard, a pending(T) collection — is what this route calls a ghost.

The trouble arrives in two blows. First, you now have two state machines describing one device — the design's registers and your model — and nothing makes them agree by construction. So the proof needs a family of extra statements, the glue invariants: “the design's valid bits correspond to my set's members,” “the design's stored addresses match my set's records,” and so on, one for every way the two machines could drift. Nobody writes these up front, because nobody knows in advance which ones the proof needs. Instead the induction discovers their absence one counterexample at a time: it finds a state where the design and the ghost disagree in some corner, you add a glue invariant to rule it out, and it finds the next corner. Entries in this repository went around this loop for days.

Second — and this is the blow that ended the v1 route — the ghost's own machinery must be ground: the solver has to build every instance of the model's rules, over every value its state could take, before it can even start searching. On the route's larger entries that construction step alone ran for 900 seconds while the solver's own report showed Solving: 0.00s — fifteen minutes of building the haystack, zero seconds of looking for the needle, and then a timeout. That line in a log is the tombstone of the ghost approach.

Now look at the right half of the figure. The window way keeps exactly one state machine — the design's — and lets the specification's promises read it through the panes. There is no second copy, therefore no drift, therefore no glue, therefore nothing for the induction to keep requesting. This is not a stylistic preference; it shows up directly in the numbers. The two cleanest proofs in this repository — the Am2901 register-file slice and this miss queue — both went through their induction at the very first strength (K=1) with zero hand-written invariants. Not because those devices are trivial, but because with windows there was literally nothing to glue.

One narrow exception survives, on purpose. A specification may keep an event-driven reference model — a model queue, a replaying referee — if every one of its rules is gated by the word refmodel. Gated machinery runs only in bounded, scripted simulation legs; it is excluded from the for-all-time claim, and the tool's report says so out loud. In other words: a ghost is allowed to be a referee for simulations; it is never allowed to be state for proofs.

4 Freedom: one contract, many designs

Why go to all this trouble? Because of what it buys: the specification stops being a description of one circuit and becomes a contract that many circuits can sign. Since promises touch hardware only through the linkage, a second, structurally different design can mount the same windows — with its own one-line-per-window adapter — and be proven against the identical promise set, unchanged.

This is the practical test of whether a specification is genuinely above the RTL. If only one implementation could ever satisfy it, the “specification” was just the RTL transcribed into logic syntax, and proving them equivalent proves only that the transcription was careful. If several substantially different implementations satisfy it, the specification captured meaning and left construction free — which is what a specification is for. And this is not an aspiration in this project; it has been measured. This repository's APB bridge has one English specification and two proven designs that differ in real structure. The miss queue exists as a flat monolith (every register at top level, comparators inline) and as a composed machine (pure-state slot modules, two comparator banks, policy in the glue) — both certified against one contract. When an entry closes with such a pair, we call it a freedom witness.

One honesty note belongs here, because it is easy to oversell. The window vocabulary is not infinitely neutral: choosing windows named slot_…(I,…) does quietly assume the design keeps per-slot state of some kind. That is accepted and stated in the methodology as “the window vocabulary constrains the implementation family.” The constraint is part of the specification's meaning — the skill is in constraining no more than the requirement itself does.

5 Semantic memory: the three honest representations

Some promises inherently relate two moments in time. “Every accepted request is later delivered” compares an event now with an event later, so something, somewhere, must remember. The ghost chapter ruled out one answer — invent spec-side memory — so the methodology must say what the honest answers are. There are exactly three, and every remembered thing in a specification must visibly pick one.

① A token, when the remembered thing is data

Addresses, tags, and payload words are never treated as numbers in this route; each is an opaque symbol — a token — like a colored marble the hardware passes along without ever looking inside. “The delivered word equals the accepted word” then needs no memory machinery at all: the proof simply checks that the marble coming out is the very same marble that went in. Identity of symbols is free, exact, and never enumerates a single bit pattern, which is why a 128-bit payload costs the proof no more than a 1-bit one.

② A structural representative, when the remembered thing is a class

Sometimes the specification wants to talk about a group: “the line” (all slots waiting on one memory address), “the episode,” “the transaction family.” The natural spelling — two slots are in the same line when their addresses are equal — is a trap, and the miss queue walked into it fully before finding the way out. Defining the group relationally means the proof's starting states must range over every possible pattern of pairwise equalities among all held addresses: with 40 slots, about 1,600 interdependent unknowns, coupled by the rule that equality is transitive. The induction step drowned in exactly that search. The repair — called R9 in the entry — is to give the class a small, concrete name that the hardware itself carries: every slot stores line_of, the index of its line's owner (the first slot of the group; an owner points at itself). Now “same line” is a comparison of two small indices, the fill finds its waiters by following pointers, and the quadratic search space simply does not exist. The general rule: when a spec needs an equivalence class, don't define it by a relation — elect a representative and store its name.

③ A bounded window, when the remembered thing is an unmet obligation

“Accepted, therefore eventually delivered” is the one memory the engine cannot honestly discharge as written: unbounded eventually is a liveness claim, and a bounded solver can neither confirm nor refute “someday.” So bare eventually is refused, with advice — and the advice is a menu of three honest spellings. Say within N cycles (that is plain finite safety, and cheap). Or say it work-conservingly: “while any obligation is unmet, the server is serving one” — a statement checkable at every single instant. Or, when neither fits, state an explicit ranking argument: a quantity that shrinks every cycle and cannot shrink forever. What is never allowed is the silent weakening of “eventually” into “reachable” — a proof that delivery is possible is not a proof that it happens.

6 Forms, and the seams where races live

The layers above describe how promises are checked. There is also the question of how they are written, and the answer starts from an observation about people: a domain expert can audit a causal sentence — “if the output is valid and the consumer is not ready, the output holds” — far more reliably than a page of logic rules. So the human-facing layer of a specification is built from causal sentences, and each recognized sentence is a form: a named template that expands into window promises. The expansion is textual and visible — it is written into the contract file where anyone can read it:

form: hold-under-backpressure(out)          — what the author writes
  ⇩ expands, in the file, to
bad(hold_wrong, T+1) :- live(T), live(T+1), val(out_valid,1,T), val(out_ready,0,T),
                        val(out_valid,0,T+1).
bad(hold_wrong, T+1) :- live(T), live(T+1), val(out_valid,1,T), val(out_ready,0,T),
                        val(out_data,V,T), not val(out_data,V,T+1).

Read the two expanded rules aloud and they are the sentence, twice: “it is wrong if, while valid and blocked, valid drops by the next cycle” and “…if the data changes by the next cycle.” Nothing in either rule says how the hardware holds — no enable, no skid buffer, no mux is mentioned — so the expansion is longer than the English but not lower-level than it. And the reason the expansion must sit visibly in the file is a matter of trust, learned the hard way: if a compiler produced the rules and nobody read them, a compiler bug would produce a wrong specification proven faithfully — a false certificate wearing a valid proof, the worst failure this route knows. Hence the rule: the contract a person reads is, literally, the contract the machine proves. A form is a scribe, never an oracle.

Now the warning that must always travel with the comfort. Causal sentences are individually easy to believe — and every race this route has ever found lived in the gap between two of them, at a single clock edge where both wanted to act. The miss queue's R3 is the canonical story. One sentence says: “a request whose line is already in flight joins that line.” Another says: “when a line's fill arrives, everyone waiting on it receives the data.” Both are plainly correct. Now let both fire on the same edge: a request arrives for a line in the very cycle its fill is landing. The joiner is admitted to a line whose fill has just passed — and will wait forever for a delivery that already happened. In fact the first version of the contract was unsatisfiable in that window: it simultaneously demanded the newcomer join (it matched an in-flight slot) and condemned the orphan it became. The repair was a resolution — “a slot whose fill is arriving this cycle is no longer joinable; such a request starts a fresh episode” — discovered only because the certificate machinery forced the seam into the open.

7 The same pictures, one level up: the two inductions

Finally, the windows are not just how promises are stated — they are what the proofs quantify over. It is worth seeing both proofs as the same maneuver.

Induction in time answers “does this hold forever?” without running forever. The argument has two halves. The base shows nothing bad can happen in the first cycle after reset. The step shows: take any state in which every promise holds — not a reachable state, not a simulated state, literally any assignment the windows could show that satisfies the promise set — run one clock edge with any inputs, and no promise breaks. If both halves hold, then no first violation can ever exist; and if there is never a first violation, there is never a violation at all. The pane labeled “ALL promises hold” in the figure's top row is literally the promise set, read through the windows of Figures 1 and 2, laid over an arbitrary state.

Induction in size plays the identical move on the other axis. The question is “does this hold at every depth — 4 slots, 40, 400?” The wrong answer is to run the certificate at each size; sizes are unbounded, and (in the user's words, now a standing rule) running them one by one loses the purpose of induction. The right answer mirrors the time step: keep a small window of two or three generic slots concrete, and replace all the other slots by a handful of free summary bits at the exact interface where the design's own logic summarizes them anyway — “someone before me is free,” “someone matched,” “someone ahead of me is ready to drain.” Constrain those free bits only by the assumed promises, and one unsatisfiability result covers a queue of every depth at once — because any real machine's window, at any size, is just one valuation of those bits. The unbounded thing is replaced by a free, promise-constrained stand-in: exactly what the time step did to “all past states.”

· The vocabulary on one card

Window — a declared, named view into design state, with a prose meaning; the specification's only state vocabulary. It is declared by the spec and defined by nobody until a linkage mounts it.  Linkage — the per-design adapter: one line per window, saying which register the pane looks at, scoped so that invisible means harmless.  Ghost — invented spec-side state, a shadow copy of the device; forbidden outside refmodel-gated simulation referees, because a copy demands glue and glue is what kills inductions.  Token — a datum as an opaque symbol; remembering it is keeping it, and equality is being the same symbol.  Representative — a class remembered as a small index the hardware carries (the owner's slot number), instead of a relation among members.  Bound — an obligation remembered as “within N,” or work-conservingly, or by a ranking argument; never as bare “eventually.”  Form — a causal sentence with a committed, visible expansion into promises; a scribe, never an oracle.  Seam — the clock edge where two causal sentences meet; where every race so far has lived; the reason the misreading pass and the resolution record exist.
